Round two · refining 07

The mini-grid meets design tokens and long text

The 3×2 grid won round one, then met the two things the panel is actually good at: naming the token a value sits on, and now holding the element's words. Mock state throughout: size on text-lg, leading on leading-6, weight and tracking off-scale, and a text too long for its field. Eight ways to say all of that in three rows.

01

Corner tick, caption on approach

Quietest possible claim: a cell whose value sits on a scale wears a 5px accent tick in its corner. Pointing at it names the token in the caption line and arms ‹ › stepping on the scroll wheel and arrow keys. Nothing widens, ever.

The tick is the whole claim: this value has a name. The caption — one line the grid already owns — does the naming, so the cell never has to. Shown here with the pointer on size.

Wins
Zero height cost and zero new furniture; the tick is exactly as loud as the information ("a scale exists here") deserves at rest.
Costs
Stepping becomes an invisible gesture — wheel or arrows on a hovered cell — which the settings page will have to teach; the tick says "a token" but never which without a visit.
Scales to
Every dense layout in the panel; the tick could mark tokenized values anywhere, one dialect for "this is on a scale".
02

This is the token pref made spatial: the "on a design token, show token" setting stops being a mode and becomes per-cell truth. Scrubbing a token cell drops it to pixels — off the rung — and the cell says so by turning plain.

Wins
The most designed-system-literate reading: the grid shows names where names exist, and the ‹ › arrows are real click targets, not a hidden gesture.
Costs
Two value languages in one grid; short step names ("6") need the caption to mean anything, and the raw number is a glance away rather than present.
Scales to
Spacing and radius grids beautifully — those are the most tokenized properties on real pages.

Numbers where the eyes tune, names where the system thinks. The shelf costs one short row when tokens exist — a page with no design system pays nothing at all.

Wins
Clean separation of concerns — scrubbing stays spatial in the grid, stepping stays nominal on the shelf — and full token names fit without squeezing cells.
Costs

Scales to
Groups with a tokenized minority. Fully-tokenized spacing would want V2 instead.
05

Cell blooms to a row

Resting, the pure grid. Clicking a cell blooms a single full-width detail row beneath — the classic anatomy with the complete stepper, token name and all — for that property only. One property gets depth; six stay shallow. Click size or leading.

The grid is the index, the bloom is the page. The full stepper — dot, label, chip, ‹ text-lg › — appears only for the property being worked, then folds away.

Wins
Full classic affordances on demand — nothing shrunk, nothing renamed — while the resting height stays 07's three rows.

Scales to
The whole panel as a universal pattern: any dense group could bloom its focused property.
06

In tok view the off-scale cells fade — the grid becomes a design-system conformance report: two named, four loose. Flip back to work in pixels.

Wins
One mental mode at a time instead of two per cell, and the tok view doubles as an audit: what here is on the system, what has drifted.
Costs
Modes hide state — in px view nothing distinguishes named values until you flip; the toggle is per-group furniture that spacing and border will then also want.
Scales to
Panel-wide as a single global toggle in the head, which may be its truest form (and the existing pref's third value, "both", dies).
07

The rung menu

A tokenized cell wears an accent baseline. Clicking it drops the whole family — sm 14, base 16, lg 18, xl 20 — as a menu with the current rung lit. Stepping is for neighbours; the menu is for going straight to a rung. Click size.

Today's stepper walks the scale one rung at a time; the menu shows the whole ladder and jumps. The accent baseline is the only resting-state cost.

Wins
Strictly more capable than the ‹ › stepper — see the ladder, jump anywhere on it — and the values beside each name teach the scale as it's used.
Costs
A dropdown is heavier furniture than the panel has ever used, needs the picker's own-root treatment to escape the panel's overflow, and hides the scrub affordance behind a click-first cell.
Scales to
Long scales especially — an 11-rung spacing family is miserable to step through and trivial to pick from.
08

The synthesis

What round two would ship: V1's corner ticks at rest, V5's bloom on click for the full stepper, the caption naming whatever the pointer touches, and the text editor open beside the panel holding the words the field truncates. Every piece has a precedent elsewhere in the extension.

Resting: 3 rows + ticks. Working: the lit cell blooms its stepper row, the caption narrates, the editor holds the words. Nothing here the panel hasn't already done somewhere.

Wins
Each state pays only for what it uses: silent page, ticked scales, one bloomed property, one text editor — and all four pieces reuse existing dialects (dot, row, popover, caption).
Costs
Four small mechanisms to build and test instead of one; the interaction spec (tick → hover → caption → click → bloom) needs writing down or it will drift.
Scales to
This is the blueprint for densifying the whole panel: grid + tick + bloom generalises to spacing, border and shadow unchanged.

A reading order: V1 and V4 are the quiet candidates (tick vs shelf); V2 and V6 make tokens the first-class value (always vs on demand); V3 refuses to hide anything and pays in height; V5 and V7 spend a click to buy full affordances; V8 is V1+V5 assembled and is the recommendation. Whatever wins, the text row is settled: truncate in place, edit long words in a popover beside the panel.
